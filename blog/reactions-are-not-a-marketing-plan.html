Reactions Are Not a Marketing Plan

By Stephanie Collins · October 13, 2026 · 5 minute read

Every marketer who has worked for a small business knows this message. It arrives at 4:45 on a Thursday.

"Can we do a post about the sale tomorrow?"

Or a competitor ran a new ad, so now we need one too. Or the owner saw something on Facebook over the weekend and wants to try it on Monday. Each request feels small. Each one feels urgent. And each one pulls the business a little further away from the plan that was supposed to grow it.

These aren't strategies. They're reactions. And reactions are not a marketing plan.

Why it happens

It usually comes from a good place. Small business owners care about everything, and they're used to deciding everything. Marketing also looks easy from the outside. Everyone has a phone, everyone posts, so everyone has an opinion about what to post.

But using social media and running marketing are about as similar as driving a car and fixing one. Being able to do the first doesn't qualify you for the second.

What reactions actually cost

The last minute post isn't free. Here's what it costs, even when it seems harmless:

  • Your budget gets scattered. Money and hours go to whatever felt urgent this week instead of what moves the numbers.
  • You never learn anything. When nothing runs long enough to measure, you can't tell what worked. Every month starts from zero.
  • Your customers get mixed messages. A brand that changes direction every week is hard to remember and harder to trust.
  • You're paying an expert to push buttons. If you hire a marketer and then tell them exactly what to post and when, you've hired an expensive assistant and thrown away the expertise.

The deal that works

The healthiest owner and marketer relationships I've had all ran on one simple agreement: you own the goals, and I own the strategy.

Here's the difference. "We need 20 more landscaping consultations booked this spring" is a goal. That's yours to set, and I'll build everything around it. "Post on Facebook every day" is a tactic. That's my call, and if the data says Facebook isn't where your customers are, I'm going to tell you.

Tell me the destination. Let me drive. Then hold me accountable for getting there, with real numbers, every month.

What a real plan looks like

At a garden center I worked with, we planned every campaign on a 52 week calendar: website, email, social, paid ads and in store, all lined up with the seasons customers actually shop. Timely posts still happened, but they fit inside the plan instead of replacing it. Over that stretch, email engagement grew 119% and transactions grew 39% year over year. That didn't come from one brilliant idea. It came from consistency.

A real plan has a few things every reaction is missing:

  • A goal you can measure
  • Campaigns scheduled before they're needed
  • A reason behind every piece of content
  • A monthly check on what worked and what didn't

Before you send that last minute idea

If you're a business owner and you feel the urge to message your marketer with something new, ask yourself three questions first:

  1. Does this serve a goal we already agreed on?
  2. What would it replace in the plan?
  3. How would we know if it worked?

If you can't answer all three, it's a reaction. Write it down and bring it to your next monthly review. A good marketer will take it seriously there, in context, where it can actually be weighed against everything else.

Hire an expert, then let them be one

You wouldn't tell your accountant which forms to file or your dentist which tool to use. Marketing deserves the same respect. Hire someone whose results you trust, give them a clear goal, and let them do the work. Your job is to run the business. Mine is to help it grow.
