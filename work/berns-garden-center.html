Berns Garden Center: from a brochure site to a store Google can actually read

A family owned garden center and landscaping company in Middletown and Beavercreek, Ohio, serving Southwest Ohio since 1956.

The short version

When I started, Berns had a dated WordPress site that sold 159 products, published about one blog post a year, had no real email platform, and was still loading an analytics tag Google had already retired. By the time I left, Berns ran on a Shopify store with more than 3,700 products in 119 shoppable collections, a Klaviyo email program, dedicated pages for each location, rebuilt analytics, and a content program that published 57 articles in about 20 months.

Every one of those pieces feeds the next. That connection is the whole point of this case study, so I'll explain it first.

How it all connects

Most business owners think of their website, their Google listing and their social media as separate things. They aren't. They're one system, and Google sits in the middle of it.

Google can only show what it can find, and it can only trust what it can understand. A page Google never crawled doesn't exist to a customer. A product without a clear title, price and photo won't appear in Shopping results. An article that doesn't answer a real question won't be picked up by Google's AI Overviews or tools like ChatGPT when someone asks "what should I plant in shade in Ohio?"

Then the loop closes. When people click, buy and leave reviews, Google treats that as proof the business is worth showing, and shows it more. A business that feeds this loop steadily grows. A business that ignores it slowly disappears from the places customers look, even if the store itself is great.

At Berns, the old site was barely feeding the loop at all. My job was to rebuild every link in it.

What I built

1. A new store on Shopify (launched February 2025)

I moved Berns from WordPress to Shopify and rebuilt the store around how gardeners actually shop. The catalog grew from 159 products in 2023 and 214 in 2024 to more than 3,700 products today. Instead of 35 generic categories, I organized them into 119 collections built around customer needs: pollinator favorites, plants for shade, trees for small spaces, evergreen screening, spring preorders, Mother's Day, and more.

Why it connects to Google: every product and collection is its own page with a title, description, price and photo. Each one is listed in the store's sitemap, which is the map Google uses to find pages. More well-described pages means more searches Berns can show up for, from "Boston fern Middletown" to "hydrangeas for part shade." Shopify also publishes structured product data (price, availability, return policy) that Google uses for Shopping results and that AI shopping tools read directly.

2. SEO: titles, descriptions, landing pages and speed of discovery

I rewrote page titles and meta descriptions, built landing pages for key services, and improved the site's structure and user experience. Search visibility rose 44%.

Why it connects: the title and description are what people see in Google before they ever visit. They are the store's first impression, written in Google's own window.

3. One page for each store

I added dedicated Beavercreek and Middletown pages, each with its own title and description, plus lead pages for landscape design and the Personal Gardener service.

Why it connects: Google ranks local businesses by relevance, distance and prominence. A page that clearly says "Berns Garden Center in Beavercreek, Ohio" gives Google a stronger reason to show that store to someone searching in Beavercreek, and supports the store's Google Business Profile.

4. Email that keeps customers coming back (Klaviyo)

I launched Klaviyo with the new store and built a regular sending cadence, lifecycle segmentation and automations. Email engagement rose 119%.

Why it connects: Google brings a customer in once. Email brings them back for free. Returning customers buy more, review more, and search for the brand by name, which is one of the strongest signals Google has that a business matters.

5. A content program Google and AI tools can use

The old site had published 7 blog posts in its lifetime. I published 57 articles in about 20 months, roughly three a month, planned around the gardening season: soil prep, spring planning, fruit trees, hedges and screens, pollinators, fall containers. Each article carries Article structured data with an author and date.

Why it connects, and why this is the AI piece: when someone asks Google or an AI assistant a gardening question, those tools build their answer from pages that clearly answer it. Local, specific, well-structured articles are exactly what they look for. Before this program, Berns had almost nothing for them to draw on. Afterward, it had a library of seasonal answers written by a local, 70-year-old garden center, which is the kind of source both Google and AI tools are designed to trust.

6. Clean measurement

I removed the retired Universal Analytics tag and rebuilt tracking on a new Google Analytics 4 property and a new Google Tag Manager container.

Why it connects: you can't improve what you can't measure. Clean data is how I knew which campaigns grew transactions 39% and which ones to cut.

7. A 52 week plan instead of last minute posts

Every campaign across website, email, social, paid media and in store was planned on a 52 week calendar tied to the gardening season, and I managed the $250K annual budget against it, including forecasting and vendor coordination. That plan is how Berns brought in more than 2,400 new customers.

What held results back, and what would have done more

No project is finished, and an honest case study says what's left. These are the gaps I see in Berns' digital presence today, and what fixing each would do.

GapWhy it mattersThe fix
Mobile speed. Google scores the homepage 63 out of 100 on mobile. The main image takes 7 seconds to appear, and the page downloads 4.5 MB.Most garden center shoppers browse on their phones. What the business could have done to get more from it

  • Invest in photography. 3,700 products deserve consistent, real photos. Product images drive Shopping results and conversions more than any copy.
  • Plan staffing for content before a transition. The content engine depended on one person. A documented calendar and a backup writer would have kept it running.
  • Give marketing a seat when decisions are made. Campaigns perform best when promotions, inventory and staffing are planned with marketing weeks ahead, not announced the day before.
  • Use paid search for the highest-value services. Landscape design and installation leads are worth far more than a single plant sale and justify a small, tightly targeted ad budget in spring.
